## 2. Narrative Premise

The game begins in 1983 as the player assumes office as the newly inaugurated President of the Republic of Varenne. The president has won a narrow and politically fragile national election, but no bloc holds an unquestioned mandate. The outgoing administration leaves disputed public accounts, weak reserves, institutional distrust, delayed disclosures, and an unresolved border crisis.

Varenne is a constitutional republic, not a personal dictatorship. The president must govern through laws, appointments, budgets, cabinet procedure, Assembly coalitions, courts, civil administration, and public legitimacy. Formal authority is substantial, but practical control is uneven. Private family choices affect public trust, while the burdens and temptations of office reshape family relationships.

The wider world is divided by a fictional Cold War. Varenne is strategically important but is not a superpower. Aid, trade, weapons, intelligence cooperation, and diplomatic recognition can relieve immediate pressure, but every offer carries political conditions, institutional risks, or long-term dependence.

The opening playable period covers the inauguration and the first phase of the administration. The long-term vision may eventually span 1983–1991; the MVP is limited to the Prologue and Chapter 1.

The central player question is:

> How much institutional, personal, and moral cost will the president accept to keep Varenne governable?

## 3. Narrative Identity and Tone

MANDATE: 1983 is an original alternate-history political thriller. It is institutionally grounded, serious but not joyless, morally ambiguous, and human rather than cynical for its own sake. It judges decisions through consequences rather than ideological scoring. Personal life matters without turning the story into a domestic soap opera, and strategic danger matters without turning the game into a tactical military simulator.

The defining emotional qualities are pressure, uncertainty, responsibility, suspicion, fatigue, hope, loyalty, compromise, regret, and occasional restrained humor. Characters may be frightened, ambitious, mistaken, brave, vain, principled, or compromised in different combinations. Institutions should feel inhabited by people rather than represented by abstract labels alone.

Avoid cartoon villains, perfect ideological factions, constant misery, constant melodrama, omniscient intelligence, modern social-media language, real-world political parties, living politicians, direct copies of real Cold War states, and borrowed characters, terminology, scenes, or choice structures from existing games.

- Legislature: National Assembly

- Currency: Varenne Crown
- Currency code: VRC
- Currency subdivision: 100 cents
- Official release-language representation: English
- In-world language names: Not required for the MVP

### Political Character

Varenne has competitive elections, a directly elected president, and a prime minister confirmed through the National Assembly. Its civil service is politically active, its independent courts operate under pressure, and its professional military retains political influence. Legal opposition parties compete within a mixed public and private economy marked by uneven regional development, state-led modernization, and a persistent fear of instability.

The presidency is powerful but constrained. The president may propose legislation, issue limited decrees, appoint ministers subject to constitutional rules, direct foreign policy, serve as commander-in-chief, request emergency authority, and refer disputes to the Constitutional Tribunal.

The president may not permanently suspend elections unilaterally, spend unlimited funds without legal authority, directly rewrite the constitution, ignore Assembly confidence indefinitely, or order unrestricted detention without legal and political consequences.

## 5. Geography and Regions

### Orsanne Metropolitan District

The capital is Varenne's administrative, financial, university, and national-media center. Dense housing surrounds ministries and commercial districts, while a large professional middle class shares the city with civil servants, students, industrial workers, and precarious renters. Its pressures include housing costs, student politics, bureaucratic strikes, intense media scrutiny, and high expectations for reform. Finance, education, administration, publishing, and professional services are its principal strengths.

### Kestrel Industrial Basin

Kestrel is a belt of heavy industry, rail yards, steelworks, machine works, power facilities, and organized labor built around aging infrastructure. It generates industrial capacity and skilled employment but depends heavily on imported fuel. Factory closures, unemployment, wage disputes, pollution, union radicalization, maintenance failures, and energy shortages divide workers, managers, municipal leaders, and state planners.

### Lydra Agricultural Plain

Lydra supplies grain, livestock, and processed food through farms, cooperatives, market towns, and nationally important transport routes. Conservative local institutions coexist with tenant organizations, agricultural unions, merchants, and reform-minded younger residents. Crop instability, fertilizer shortages, fuel rationing, rural debt, unequal land ownership, and food-price politics create competing interests within the region.

### Roven Marches

Roven is the eastern border region: a mixture of towns, farms, mines, military bases, strategic roads, and communities with family ties across the frontier. Its pressures include border incidents, smuggling, displacement, military presence, suspicion of foreign influence, and weak civilian infrastructure. Residents differ sharply over security, commerce, regional autonomy, and relations with Dravica.

Every region must retain a distinct political character, economic strengths, competing internal interests, and grievances. Regional consequences may diverge from national averages. No region is inherently disloyal, backward, inferior, or reducible to one ideology.

## 6. Historical Timeline

### 1946 — The Republican Settlement

The royal government collapses after prolonged economic crisis and division within the armed forces. Negotiators representing civilian parties, regional administrations, labor organizations, officers, and remaining royal institutions agree to a republican constitution rather than risk civil war. The presidency, National Assembly, Constitutional Tribunal, and modern regional administration emerge from the settlement.

### 1951–1958 — Reconstruction and State Expansion

National rail, energy, housing, and literacy programs expand. State-owned industries become central to reconstruction and national development. Employment and access to services improve, but uneven investment leaves regional inequality unresolved.

### 1962 — The Barracks Crisis

During mass unrest, senior officers pressure the civilian government to change policy and leadership. Negotiation, loyalist resistance, public opposition, and division within the armed forces allow the government to survive. The military formally returns to barracks, but civilian leaders remain alert to military political influence. The crisis did not become a completed coup and did not formally replace civilian government.

### 1968 — The Orsanne Compact

Major political parties agree to electoral and judicial reforms. The Constitutional Tribunal gains stronger review powers, and regional governors receive limited administrative autonomy. The Compact becomes both a symbol of negotiated reform and a target for those who believe it preserved too much elite power.

### 1973–1976 — Accelerated Industrial Program

The government borrows heavily to expand industry and energy capacity. Growth initially rises, but corruption, rushed procurement, maintenance failures, and foreign debt accumulate beneath optimistic official reporting.

### 1978 — The Winter of Accounts

A financial investigation exposes hidden liabilities and procurement fraud. Several officials resign, but no full institutional reckoning follows. Rival parties selectively use the findings, records remain incomplete, and public trust declines.

### 1980 — The Coalition Fracture

The governing coalition splits over austerity, subsidies, and emergency policing. Cabinet instability becomes normal. The opposition grows in strength but remains divided over constitutional strategy and economic policy.

### 1982 — Failed Harvest and Reserve Decline

Agricultural output falls as fuel imports become more expensive. Government reserves decline, arrears spread, and official economic reporting becomes increasingly disputed.

### Early 1983 — The Narrow Election

The player wins a closely contested presidential election. No political bloc receives an unquestioned mandate, the National Assembly remains fragmented, and the outgoing administration delays several disclosures. A deadly border incident occurs shortly before inauguration.

Exact calendar dates, vote shares, seat counts, and other numerical election results remain undefined until production content requires them.

## 7. Political Institutions

### Presidency

The nationally elected presidency controls agenda-setting, appointments, diplomacy, and emergency proposals. Its effectiveness depends on legal authority, Assembly cooperation, public legitimacy, cabinet cohesion, and actual state capacity.

### Office of the Prime Minister

The prime minister coordinates ministers, manages Assembly confidence, and controls much of daily government procedure. The office may become the president's partner, rival, political shield, or successor threat depending on choices and relationships.

### Council of Ministers

The Council is the collective executive body. Ministers bring competing professional duties, constituencies, ambitions, and political loyalties. Cabinet unity must be earned and must never be assumed.

### National Assembly

The fragmented legislature controls ordinary legislation, budget approval, and government confidence. It contains parties, regional caucuses, independents, professional networks, and factional blocs that do not always vote predictably.

### Constitutional Tribunal

The Tribunal reviews emergency authority, election disputes, executive procedure, and constitutional conflict. It is independent in law but vulnerable to procedural delay, appointment pressure, political campaigns, and attacks on its legitimacy.

### Civil Service Commission

The Commission oversees senior administrative appointments and protects professional continuity. It can defend competence and legality, but it can also shelter patronage, delay elected priorities, or resist change through procedure.

### Armed Forces General Staff

The General Staff is the professional command institution and is formally subordinate to civilian authority. It is divided by generation, service branch, doctrine, personal loyalties, and competing memories of 1962.

### National Security Directorate

The Directorate produces domestic and foreign intelligence from incomplete and contested sources. It faces rivalry among its departments and the possibility of internal compromise.

### National Broadcasting Service

The NBS is the public broadcaster, legally accountable to public institutions rather than directly to the president. Appointments, budgets, access, and emergency rules expose it to pressure from every major political camp.

## 8. Political Factions

The three MVP factions are broad political networks, not perfectly unified parties. Their memberships overlap with formal parties, institutions, professions, and regions.

### Civic Renewal League

- Base: Urban professionals, reformist civil servants, younger voters, lawyers, educators, and small-business owners.
- Priorities: Constitutional reform, anti-corruption, administrative competence, judicial independence, measured economic modernization, and press protections.
- Internal divisions: Gradual institutionalists, faster democratic reformers, and market-oriented modernizers.
- Fears: Military intervention, executive overreach, reform collapse, and economic disorder discrediting democracy.

### National Stewardship Union

- Base: Traditional conservatives, senior business networks, security institutions, rural notables, veterans, and state-administration loyalists.
- Priorities: Order, national sovereignty, military readiness, central authority, social continuity, and protection of strategic industries.
- Internal divisions: Constitutional conservatives, security hardliners, and economic nationalists.
- Fears: State fragmentation, foreign dependence, radical labor action, and weak civilian leadership.

### Workers’ Commonwealth

- Base: Industrial unions, public-sector workers, mining communities, cooperative organizers, left intellectuals, and lower-income urban districts.
- Priorities: Employment, wages, price stability, public services, industrial investment, and labor representation.
- Internal divisions: Parliamentary social democrats, militant trade unionists, and state-socialist organizers.
- Fears: Austerity, unprotected privatization, anti-union policing, and foreign control of national industry.

Every faction must contain competent and flawed figures, regional variation, and internal disagreement. No faction is the universal moral answer. Factions remember promises, betrayals, appointments, concessions, exclusions, and repression.

## 9. Economic and Social Foundation

Varenne has a mixed economy combining state-owned heavy industry, private agriculture and cooperatives, small and medium private businesses, regulated banking, strategic import dependence, public fuel and food subsidies, large infrastructure obligations, foreign debt, regional inequality, and strong labor organization.

At inauguration, the country faces weak reserves, food-price pressure, fuel-import costs, factory maintenance failures, youth unemployment, rural debt, government payment arrears, disputed fiscal reporting, procurement corruption, and low investor and consumer confidence.

Its social tensions include urban versus rural priorities, workers versus management, center versus regions, security versus civil liberties, traditional authority versus generational change, and public expectations versus limited state capacity.

This document establishes qualitative canon only. Exact formulas, weights, thresholds, and starting numeric values belong in `SYSTEMS_DESIGN.md`.

## 10. Military, Police, and Intelligence

### Armed Forces

The army is the dominant service, supported by smaller air and naval branches. Readiness is uneven, with fuel and maintenance shortages affecting every service. The armed forces possess a strong professional identity and an institutional memory of the Barracks Crisis. Officers include constitutionalists, nationalists, and interventionists, but the military is not automatically disloyal.

### Presidential Guard

The Guard is a small protective formation institutionally separate from the ordinary police. Its proximity to the presidency makes it politically sensitive, but it is neither a private army nor an unrestricted instrument of personal rule.

### National Police

The police are responsible for ordinary internal security and public order. Professionalism varies by unit and region. Their strong urban presence places them under intense political pressure during strikes, demonstrations, shortages, and emergency measures.

### National Security Directorate

The Directorate combines domestic and foreign intelligence responsibilities. Rival departments compete for access and credibility. Source reliability is incomplete, and a foreign service or internal patronage network may have compromised part of the institution. The Directorate has no magical knowledge.

Intelligence assessments must use these confidence categories:

- Confirmed
- Highly credible
- Probable
- Unverified
- Disputed
- Likely fabricated
- Deliberate disinformation

## 11. The Fictional Cold War

### Helian Accord

The Helian Accord links maritime commercial powers. Many members hold competitive elections and favor private capital, development loans, security partnerships, trade access, and intelligence cooperation. Alignment can bring market access and investment, but may impose fiscal conditions, political influence, military-access requests, and exposure to foreign corporate interests.

Its leading power is the Commonwealth of Caldris.

### Dromir Pact

The Dromir Pact links centralized party states through state-directed industry, military and energy assistance, intelligence cooperation, guaranteed procurement markets, and ideological diplomacy. Alignment can provide rapid material support, but may impose political conditions, security dependence, restricted diplomatic flexibility, domestic factional pressure, and institutional penetration.

Its leading power is the Union of Dromir Republics.

Varenne is formally non-aligned. Non-alignment does not mean neutrality from consequences, immunity from influence, or equal distance on every issue.

### Dravica

Varenne's eastern neighbor is associated with the Dromir Pact and shares the disputed Roven frontier. Cross-border communities and transport links complicate the dispute. Dravica publicly denies aggressive intentions while applying diplomatic, economic, and intelligence pressure.

### Belvar Republic

The northern commercial neighbor is formally neutral and serves as an important rail and banking partner. Its leaders seek profit and stability more consistently than ideological alignment.

### Republic of Cyrane

The southern maritime state has close trade ties with the Helian Accord. It offers port access and fuel agreements while competing with Varenne for regional diplomatic influence.

These foreign states are original composites within the fictional setting, not direct replacements for specific real countries.

## 12. MVP Crisis Threads

These threads define foundations for the Prologue and Chapter 1. They are not complete production scenarios, dialogue, branch graphs, or final resolutions.

### The Supply Emergency

Food and fuel shortages collide with disputed reserve figures, subsidy pressure, transport bottlenecks, labor unrest, and cabinet disagreement. Possible policy directions include emergency imports, rationing, subsidies, price controls, borrowing, an anti-corruption inquiry, regional relief, union negotiation, and security enforcement. No response is cost-free.

### The Roven Border Incident

A deadly incident near the Dravican border produces conflicting military and civilian reports. Responsibility is disputed, families and displaced people cross the frontier, and pressure for mobilization competes with the risk of escalation and an opportunity for diplomacy. Objective truth must not be revealed immediately.

### The Lantern File

Fragmentary intelligence suggests a leak or influence network inside the government. Evidence quality is mixed, and foreign penetration, domestic patronage, deliberate disinformation, or some combination remain plausible. Rivals may exploit the inquiry; overreaction may damage institutions, while underreaction may allow further compromise. This baseline does not establish a final culprit.

### Cabinet Authority Conflict

Prime-ministerial authority conflicts with the presidential agenda as finance and security priorities divide ministers. Assembly confidence and collective responsibility make resignation, compromise, procedural obstruction, and public division credible risks.

### First-Family Conflict

The first family encounters immediate public scrutiny. At least one family member opposes a contemplated emergency measure, while another faces a conflict between private opportunity and public ethics. The spouse has an independent public identity, and no family member functions as a passive approval meter.

### Election Pressure Thread

The presidential election has ended, but local and Assembly contests continue to shape political behavior. Allegations from the recent election remain active, and parties prepare for future contests. The MVP does not implement a complete national election engine.

### Chapter 1 Climax

Multiple crisis threads converge around imperfect institutional paths. Earlier promises, memories, faction relationships, regional conditions, and economic decisions alter available choices and consequences. The structure must support three distinct MVP route outcomes, none determined by one isolated choice. The final climax scene is not defined here.

### President

The newly inaugurated president has a player-selected background. Public authority exceeds practical control, and the office requires balancing family, coalition, constitutional duty, and legacy.

### Spouse

The spouse is an independent adult public figure with a personal cause and public reputation. They may support the presidency while opposing specific decisions and must never exist only as a romance or approval meter.

### Adult Daughter

The adult daughter has an independent profession or public-interest commitment. She is attentive to civil liberties and institutional credibility and is capable of principled judgment as well as personal error.

### Adult Son

The adult son has an independent career path and faces pressure to benefit from the family name. He may understand economic or logistical realities differently from the president and is neither entitled by definition nor immune to temptation.

### Adult Sibling

The adult sibling shares a long personal history with the president and has political or business connections. Their relationship creates potential conflicts involving loyalty, influence, and equal application of law.

No family member is automatically morally correct or automatically corrupt. Family choices create public and private consequences, and family members remember treatment, promises, protection, and betrayal. Names and selected portraits persist through save and load. Stable IDs and file paths never use customized names.

Affairs, pregnancy, death, and scandal are not universal canon. Any such arc must be route-dependent, adult, non-explicit, and separately approved.

- Reputation: Formally public but vulnerable to government pressure
- Tone: Official, procedural, and cautious

- Form: Labor and industrial newspaper
- Audience: Unions and industrial communities
- Reputation: Strong workplace reporting with openly political editorials
- Tone: Direct, collective, and skeptical of austerity

- Audience: Business, security, veterans, and provincial elites

## 17. Objective Canon and Deliberate Uncertainty

### Objective Canon

- Varenne is a constitutional republic.
- The game begins in 1983.
- The player won a narrow presidential election.
- National reserves are weak.
- The National Assembly is fragmented.
- The Roven border is disputed.
- The first family is customizable and has no canonical production names.
- The Barracks Crisis did not formally replace civilian government.

### Deliberately Uncertain at MVP Start

- Full responsibility for the Roven Border Incident.
- The exact scale of reserve manipulation.
- Whether the Lantern File represents foreign penetration, domestic patronage, deliberate disinformation, or a combination.
- Which senior officials knew about hidden liabilities.
- Whether current military political coordination exists.
- Whether foreign aid offers are coordinated with internal political actors.

Narrative and intelligence content must preserve these uncertainties fairly. Evidence may narrow possibilities, but ordinary player-facing text must not reveal developer-only truth or present one unproven explanation as objective canon.

## 18. Continuity Rules

Every production scenario must respect:

- Political date and chapter.
- Current office holders and cabinet membership.
- Character location and life status.
- Resignation, dismissal, imprisonment, and exile.
- Family role and customized names.
- Relationship and faction memories.
- Publicly known, privately known, and classified information.
- War and border status.
- Assembly and constitutional status.
- Media exposure.
- Prior player choices and resolved consequences.

No character may reference information they do not know. No resigned or dismissed official may speak as an active minister unless restored. No family member may receive a fixed canonical production name. No rumor, allegation, propaganda claim, or intelligence hypothesis automatically becomes objective canon.

## 19. Content and Rating Boundaries

MANDATE: 1983 targets a Teen audience.

At a non-graphic level, content may include political betrayal, corruption, bribery, blackmail, espionage, protests, imprisonment, coup fears, assassination attempts, war, death, implied consensual adult affairs, marriage conflict, and period-appropriate alcohol or smoking references.

Content must exclude explicit sexual content, nudity, sexual violence, incest, romantic content involving minors, graphic gore, graphic torture, detailed real-world operational instructions for terrorism, assassination, coups, sabotage, or military operations, real extremist propaganda, living politicians as characters, real political parties, and dehumanizing portrayals of real protected groups.

No character's appearance, region, class, disability, or background may serve as shorthand for moral worth.

## 20. Originality and Non-Canon Rules

Countries, institutions, parties, factions, characters, events, wars, laws, dialogue, media, endings, terminology, and visual insignia must be fully original.

The following are prohibited:

- Renamed copies of *Suzerain* characters, countries, or parties.
- Matching *Suzerain* scene sequences.
- Copied choice wording, newspaper concepts or headlines, endings, or distinctive terminology.
- Direct one-to-one fictional aliases for real countries.
- Real-person portrait references.
- Real military insignia.

Genre similarity is acceptable. Distinctive creative expression must remain original. Material not approved as objective canon, deliberate uncertainty, or a route-dependent possibility is non-canon and must not be treated as established fact.
