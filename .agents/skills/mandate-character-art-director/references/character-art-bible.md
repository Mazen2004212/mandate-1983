## Visual Identity

Use semi-realistic editorial illustration for an alternate-history 1980s political thriller. Draw from political-magazine portrait traditions without copying a particular publication or artist. Combine restrained oil-painted surfaces, subtle print texture, cinematic but plausible lighting, serious human presence, and original fictional faces.

Keep realistic anatomy, expressive but controlled faces, tangible fabric and materials, and clear silhouettes. The complete cast should feel authored for one world.

## Emotional Tone

Communicate authority, fatigue, suspicion, conviction, social status, professional identity, private vulnerability, and political pressure according to the character and scene.

Do not lock a character into exaggerated anger, melodramatic fear, or villainous shadow. Let subtle posture, gaze, and tension reveal private emotion beneath public control.

## Composition Standard

- Use a 4:5 aspect ratio.
- Create masters at 1200 x 1500 or larger.
- Frame characters bust-up or waist-up.
- Prefer a three-quarter pose while varying it when narrative context justifies a different angle.
- Maintain consistent approximate head scale and camera height across a set.
- Keep the face approximately 38% to 52% of portrait height.
- Reserve a safe crop region around the head, chin, key clothing, insignia, and accessories.
- Use a neutral or contextual textured background that remains restrained.
- Test readability on dark interface and light paper surfaces.

Leave useful negative space for responsive layouts. Do not make a composition depend on one exact UI crop.

## Lighting Standard

Use a soft key, controlled rim, and restrained shadows. Favor warm office light for civilian political interiors and cooler intelligence or military light where context supports it. Keep direction and facial modeling consistent across one character's expression set.

Avoid neon rims, overexposure, crushed facial shadows, glamour photography, and expression variants whose lighting makes them look like unrelated sessions.

## Color Standard

Keep portraits compatible with the interface palette:

- Ink Black `#171B1A`
- Coal `#252B29`
- Deep Green `#17352E`
- Navy `#24364B`
- Burgundy `#6A2935`
- Brass `#B68A45`
- Paper `#E7DEC9`
- Warm White `#F9F6EE`

Use character-specific accent colors sparingly. Preserve skin-tone accuracy and separation from backgrounds instead of forcing every portrait into the same tint.

## Expression Standard

### Neutral

Relax the brow and jaw, keep a steady eye line, close the mouth naturally, and use balanced posture and lighting. Neutral must still express personality.

### Supportive

Soften the brow and eyes, permit a restrained mouth lift, open the posture slightly, and use a small head inclination. Avoid broad promotional smiles.

### Concerned

Draw the brows inward or upward subtly, focus the eyes, compress or part the mouth slightly, and introduce modest forward tension. Keep the pose plausible.

### Angry

Lower and tighten the brow, intensify the gaze, set the jaw, and make posture firmer. Avoid bared teeth, extreme redness, and theatrical distortion.

### Suspicious

Narrow or redirect the gaze, create slight asymmetric brow tension, close the mouth, and shift the head angle minimally. Avoid caricatured squinting.

### Exhausted

Lower the posture and eyelids slightly, relax mouth tension, show age-appropriate fatigue, and reduce lighting intensity without obscuring the face. Do not imply illness unless required.

Across every expression, preserve face structure, age, hair, camera angle, crop, clothing continuity, and lighting direction.

## Age Standard

Represent age through facial structure, skin texture, hair, posture, clothing, and physical fatigue. Use restraint and consistency between variants. Do not smooth every face into an apparent age of 25 to 35, and do not exaggerate older characters into caricatures.

## Professional Visual Language

### President

Convey authority and controlled public presentation while supporting customizable appearance. Provide coherent formal and crisis clothing variants. Preserve presidential identity across every option.

### Cabinet Ministers

Express professional identity through role-appropriate materials, posture, accessories, and restrained individual color motifs. Keep the cabinet visually distinct without turning portfolios into costumes.

### Military

Use fictional insignia, disciplined posture, and distinguishable fictional service branches. Keep medals and ranks coherent. Do not copy real uniforms or operational equipment exactly.

### Intelligence

Use restrained clothing, economical accessories, and controlled expressions. Do not rely on darkness, scars, or sinister styling as automatic villain coding.

### Opposition

Represent diverse class, regional, ideological, and professional backgrounds. Do not signal opposition through automatic disorder, menace, or moral ugliness.

### Journalists

Use practical period clothing. Include notebooks, press badges, microphones, or cameras only where context supports them, using fictional marks and plausible 1980s designs.

### Family

Show personal warmth, social and political individuality, and meaningful variation. Do not reduce family members to decoration or define them only through the president.

### Foreign Leaders and Diplomats

Create distinct fictional national visual languages with original flags, emblems, and clothing cues. Do not directly reproduce real countries, leaders, or insignia.

## Diversity

Build meaningful variation in face structure, skin tone, age, build, hair texture, grooming, clothing, region, and social class. Include disability where narratively appropriate and portray it accurately and respectfully.

Do not generate diversity by recoloring one face. Do not stereotype morality, loyalty, intelligence, criminality, or political alignment through ethnicity, disability, scars, body type, clothing, or appearance.

## Clothing Reference

### Civilian and Presidential

Use conservative suits, three-piece suits, professional dresses, blouses, skirts, overcoats, scarves, modest jewelry, period glasses, fictional party pins, and government badges. Presidential variants may include formal coats, summer suits, a former-officer ceremonial jacket, an open-collar crisis outfit, and a heavy winter coat.

### Diplomatic and Academic

Use tailored but restrained formalwear, regionally distinct fictional textiles, practical academic jackets, knitwear, blouses, skirts, period eyewear, and contextual accessories. Avoid costume-like national shorthand.

### Military

Use coherent fictional service uniforms, staff coats, field jackets, ceremonial uniforms, medals, and ranks. Keep branch conventions internally consistent and avoid exact real-world replicas.

### Working Class and Media

Use plausible work jackets, knitwear, shirts, practical coats, office separates, press outerwear, and contextual equipment. Clothing should show use without equating poverty with dirt or disorder.

### Family

Balance personal taste, profession, class, age, and public scrutiny. Give each adult an independent visual identity and appropriate formal, domestic, or crisis variants where the story needs them.

Reject modern slim-fit tailoring, current tactical gear, smartphones, modern eyewear and logos, contemporary hairstyles, and twenty-first-century camouflage.

## Forbidden Traits

- Real-person resemblance
- Modern clothing or technology
- Real military insignia or party symbols
- Cartoon anatomy or fashion-model posing
- Generic generated faces or identical-face reuse
- Impossible medals, accessories, or garment construction
- Unwanted text, logos, signatures, or watermarks
- Photorealistic or cartoon outliers that break the shared style
